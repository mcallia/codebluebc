PULSE · STEADY

CHAPTER 01

Every tap in B.C. starts in the sky — and in the snow.

Winter storms bank snow in the mountains. Spring and summer melt pays it out — slowly, steadily — into creeks, rivers, lakes and underground aquifers. That meltwater becomes salmon habitat, irrigation for farms, and drinking water for most communities in the province.

Think of the snowpack as B.C.’s largest reservoir — a savings account that fills all winter and pays for everything all summer. No dam we could ever build comes close to its size.

A watershed is simply all the land that drains into one body of water — the mountainsides, forests, wetlands and floodplains that catch, filter, store and slowly release it. Healthy watersheds do this for free, forever.

When they’re healthy.

CHAPTER 02

The patient has a fever.

A warming climate is destabilizing the water cycle itself: snow that doesn’t come, melt that comes too early, rain that arrives all at once. B.C. now swings between too little water and far too much — sometimes in the same year.

And when the water comes back, it comes back angry. The atmospheric river of November 2021 put Sumas Prairie under water, cut every highway to the coast, and became the costliest disaster in B.C. history — insured losses alone topped $675 million, and the full bill ran into the billions.

Same story with fire: clearcut valleys shed water faster, burn hotter, and flood harder. Drought, fire and flood aren’t three emergencies. They’re one patient with three symptoms.

“Farmers can’t keep reliving the same drought emergency year after year.” — Dave Zehnder, rancher, Columbia Valley

CHAPTER 03

Meanwhile, we’re giving the water away.

While communities ration, B.C. sells industrial access to fresh water at the lowest rates in Canada — and enforcement of the rules is thin.

The province’s ten biggest industrial permit holders can access more than 500 billion litres a year for under $1 million, combined. Fracking operations in the drought-stricken northeast draw more water every year. Nobody can even say precisely how much water new data centres use — reporting isn’t required.

And when polluters get caught? Fines are routinely smaller than the cost of following the rules. 55% of British Columbians say enforcement is “not tough enough.” A decade after the Mount Polley tailings dam collapse, the same dam is proposed to get bigger.

CHAPTER 04

What’s on the line

Water is life — and in B.C. it’s also dinner, payroll, culture and identity.

Food & farms

From Okanagan orchards to Fraser Valley dairies, B.C. food security runs on reliable water. Threats to food crops now rank among British Columbians’ top freshwater worries.

Drinking water

Metro Vancouver is openly studying how to keep taps running for the next million residents; smaller systems from Hudson’s Hope to Courtenay face big bills and hard limits.

Jobs & the blue economy

B.C.’s watershed sector supports 47,900 jobs and $5 billion in GDP — restoration crews, guides, farmers, brewers, builders of natural defences.

Rights & reconciliation

Indigenous nations have stewarded these waters since time immemorial and hold constitutionally protected rights. Watershed security and reconciliation flow together.

A pulse worth protecting: when conditions line up, rivers roar back — the Coquitlam River just recorded a historic return of sockeye. The patient responds to treatment.

CHAPTER 06

The crash cart is ready. This is the response.

None of this is mysterious. The CodeBlue plan — and the BC Watershed Security Coalition’s roadmap — lay out exactly what the moment requires:

  1. Charge industry fairly for water — modern rates would generate $100 million+ every year, dedicated to watershed security and drought preparedness.
  2. Get tough on wasters and polluters — real enforcement, real penalties, and full implementation of the decade-old Water Sustainability Act.
  3. Put local people in charge of local water — resourced watershed boards where communities, First Nations and local governments make decisions together.
  4. Build the fund that lasts — grow B.C.’s Watershed Security Fund toward $1 billion, so restoration and monitoring never again depend on one budget cycle.
“The challenge isn’t a lack of solutions — it’s a lack of delivery.” — Coree Tull, Chair, BC Watershed Security Coalition

Compare the price tags: a fully funded watershed security system costs a fraction of a single flood year. Prevention is the cheapest medicine we’re not buying.
